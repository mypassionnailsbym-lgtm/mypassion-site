Why are my nails soft after gel polish or gel removal?

You have your nail coating removed, touch your nail and wonder: “Why is it so soft? It used to feel hard.”

This feeling can be worrying, especially if you have worn gel polish or gel for a long time. But softness alone does not automatically mean your nails have been damaged or that your nail stylist has made a mistake.

Once the product is removed, its extra support disappears, and your natural nail plate may be more flexible than you remember. Water, its previous condition, and the preparation and removal methods also affect how it behaves. To understand the difference, let’s start with the structure of the nail.

A nail is not one uniform layer

The nail plate consists of many tightly connected, flattened cells filled with keratin — a protein that also makes up our hair.

It has three main parts:

  • the dorsal layer — the outer part that we touch and that nail products are applied to;
  • the intermediate layer — in the middle;
  • the ventral layer — the underside, in contact with the nail bed, which is the tissue beneath the nail.

These are not three individual “sheets”. Each part contains many layers of cells. Their connections and the arrangement of keratin work together to give the nail plate its strength.

Imagine thin sheets firmly bonded together. Together, they form a stronger structure. If some of that structure is removed or starts to separate, its strength changes too.

Natural nails also vary from person to person. A healthy nail plate is not necessarily thick and rigid — it can be naturally thin and flexible.

Why does a nail stylist lightly buff the nail plate during preparation?

For many nail systems, preparation includes gently buffing the nail surface to remove the shine, following the manufacturer’s instructions. This helps prepare the surface for product adhesion.

The aim is not to file away the entire outer layer or create deep scratches. Buffing should be superficial, controlled and adapted to the condition of the nail plate.

One point is worth clarifying: shine is not a separate coating that can be removed without affecting the nail. Buffing changes the surface through abrasion. That is why the tool, pressure and number of strokes matter.

Correct, gentle preparation is not the same as overfiling a nail. Using a file does not, in itself, mean the service has been carried out incorrectly.

Not every system requires the same preparation either — some manufacturers do not recommend buffing the natural nail plate for their products. Good practice means adapting the procedure to the product and the nails, rather than always repeating the same steps.

Why is the difference so noticeable after removal?

For several weeks, you have been touching a nail covered with a hardened material. This extra layer provides mechanical support and limits bending. The effect can be particularly noticeable with gel.

Once the product is removed, only the natural nail plate remains. If it was thin and flexible before, it may still be that way after removal.

It is a little like taking a rigid case off a phone: without it, the whole thing immediately feels more delicate. This comparison explains the change in sensation, but it cannot tell you whether the nail itself is healthy.

The hardness of a gel-covered nail does not mean that the natural nail plate underneath has become thicker. It is therefore better to compare its condition with how it was before the treatment, rather than with the rigidity of the hardened material.

Water also changes nail flexibility

The nail is not a completely impermeable surface. It absorbs water and becomes softer and easier to bend after soaking. You may have noticed this after a long bath or washing the dishes.

Assessing the nail plate immediately after soaking can therefore be misleading. Take another look once your hands and nails are dry, without repeatedly bending them “to test them”.

Frequent cycles of wetting and drying can also encourage nails to split into layers. Temporary softness after contact with water and damage to the nail plate are different things, although they can happen at the same time.

When can softness indicate genuine weakening?

The nail plate can become excessively thin during preparation or product removal. Overfiling, scraping and peeling off the material can all play a part.

When you peel off a piece of gel polish, some of the nail’s surface layers may come away with it. The plate can then become thinner and more prone to bending, splitting and cracking.

A small study described weakness, brittleness and thinning in the nails of five people after gel polish manicures and removal involving acetone and manual peeling. These were specific cases — not proof that every nail treatment must cause such changes (Chen and colleagues, 2012, “Nail damage from gel polish manicure”).

The history of the nail plate matters too. The same section of nail remains through successive appointments until it grows out. If it was previously thinned, it does not regain its thickness before the next application.

That is why not every soft nail can be attributed to a stylist’s mistake, but softness should not automatically be dismissed either. The nails’ condition before the treatment, what happened while the product was worn and how it was removed all need to be considered.

What about acetone?

Nail polish removers can dry out the nails and surrounding skin. This may contribute to brittleness and splitting. Dryness is not the same as temporary softness after soaking, however, so not every change can be explained by saying: “It’s because of the acetone.”

The removal method should suit the particular product. Not every gel dissolves in acetone, and material that does not come away should never be forcibly peeled off.

The whole process matters: the correct remover, the time it is left to work and gentle handling of the nail plate. This applies both to salon removal and to attempts to remove products at home.

What can you do after removal?

Start with simple, regular care:

  • Keep your nails shorter, so they are less likely to catch and bend during everyday activities.
  • Massage cream into your hands, nails and the skin around them, especially after washing. Nail and cuticle oil can complement this routine.
  • Wear gloves for washing dishes and cleaning, to limit prolonged contact with water and detergents.
  • Do not pick off peeling layers or leftover product.
  • Do not aggressively polish the surface in an attempt to remove every uneven area — you could thin the plate further.

If your nails are visibly damaged, do not cover the problem with another application without having their condition assessed. Tell your stylist what you have noticed and whether you feel any discomfort.

Can the nail plate rebuild itself in a few days?

Care helps protect the nails and reduce dryness, but it cannot replace layers filed away or torn off with the product. The thinned section needs to grow out gradually.

Fingernails grow by around 3 mm per month on average. Their appearance and comfort may therefore improve before the damaged section has fully grown out.

There is no single deadline by which all nails should return to their previous condition. This depends, among other things, on the extent of the changes, the growth rate and everyday exposure to trauma.

When should you consult a dermatologist?

If softness is accompanied by pain, redness, swelling, unusual colour changes or the nail plate lifting away from the nail bed, consult a doctor. The same applies if the problem persists or gets worse.

Not every nail change is caused by a nail treatment. A stylist can notice concerning signs and decide not to carry out a service, but cannot replace a dermatological diagnosis.

After gel polish or gel removal, you do not need to expect your natural nail to stay as rigid as it was with the product on. What matters most is whether it has returned to its natural flexibility or whether new changes need attention. Softness alone does not provide the answer — but understanding the nail plate’s structure and what it has been through helps you find it.
